About this page

Bike Fit Visualizer draws a rider on a bike to scale, from your body measurements and the bike’s geometry, and animates the pedal stroke so you can see how the two fit together. The readouts under the drawing measure the fit as it moves (joint angles, reach, standover and more), and mark each one typical, marginal or out of range.

Use it to understand a bike before you buy it, to compare sizes, or to see what a longer stem or a lower saddle would change. It is a model built from averages and published rules of thumb, not a substitute for a fitting with a professional, who can watch how you actually ride.

Body measurements

Your Body starts from your height and sex and fills in an average length for each part of the body, as a share of your height.

Men. The male shares follow the body segment table of Drillis and Contini (1966), as reproduced in David A. Winter’s Biomechanics and Motor Control of Human Movement, a standard reference in biomechanics. In it the ankle sits at 3.9% of height, the knee at 28.5%, the hip at 53.0% and the shoulder at 81.8%. The thigh, lower leg and torso are the gaps between them, and the neck and head make up the last 18.2%. Two figures are the page’s own: the inseam, and the forearm, which runs from the elbow to the middle of the grip rather than to the wrist.

Women. The female shares come from ANSUR II, the U.S. Army’s 2012 anthropometric survey (Gordon and colleagues, 2014), which measured 1,986 women. Each share is the average of every woman’s measurement divided by her own height:

  • Inseam: crotch height, 48.0%.
  • Thigh: hip (trochanterion) height less knee (lateral femoral epicondyle) height, 23.3%.
  • Lower leg: knee height less ankle (lateral malleolus) height, 24.8%.
  • Upper arm: shoulder to elbow (acromion–radiale), 19.1%.
  • Forearm: elbow to wrist (radiale–stylion), 14.8%, plus the part of the hand up to the middle of the grip, measured the same way as the male figure: 19.1% in all.
  • Foot length: 15.1%.

In the survey the women’s ankle sits at 3.85% of height and the shoulder at 82.0%, within 0.2% of the figures the page uses for both sexes, so the torso is what is left between them, 29.8%. The survey’s subjects are soldiers, who are younger and fitter than the population as a whole, but body proportions vary much less than weight does.

These are averages, and people vary a good deal around them. Choose Enter my own to replace them with your own measurements. Legs and torso always add up to your height, so lengthening one shortens the other.

Bike recommendations

Get Recommendation picks a frame size for your body, the kind of bike and your riding style, then sets up the saddle and cockpit on it.

Frame sizes. Each size is a consensus of current stock geometry charts (2024 to 2026 models). It is written in the round figures frames are built to: top tubes and seat tubes in whole centimeters, head tubes and chainstays to 5 mm, angles to half a degree, with stock forks, stems and crank lengths.

  • Road: endurance frames, from Specialized (Roubaix), Trek (Domane), Cannondale (Synapse), Canyon (Endurace) and Giant (Defy), with Moots, Mosaic, Enve and Fairlight. They were compared with race frames: Specialized Tarmac, Trek Madone, Cannondale SuperSix EVO, Canyon Ultimate and Giant TCR.
  • Gravel: Specialized Diverge, Trek Checkpoint, Canyon Grizl, Giant Revolt, Santa Cruz Stigmata, Cervélo Aspero and Salsa Warbird, with Moots, Enve, Ritchey, Fairlight and Open. Race frames (Crux, Aspero, Grail, Checkmate, Ostro Gravel, Kaius, Stigmata) were compared with adventure frames (Grizl, Diverge, Checkpoint, Topstone, Libre, Revolt).
  • Mountain: cross-country and trail hardtails: Specialized Chisel and Fuse, Trek Procaliber and Roscoe, Canyon Exceed, Giant XTC, Santa Cruz Chameleon, Kona Honzo, Salsa Timberjack and Ritchey Ultra, compared with race hardtails (Epic HT, Exceed, Scale, Alma, Highball). The two smallest sizes are on 27.5 in wheels.

Choosing the size.

  1. The size starts from your height. It moves toward a longer frame if your torso and arms are long for your height and toward a shorter one if they are short (half of the difference counts), as makers advise for riders between sizes.
  2. Your inseam can move you down, but no more than one size below the one for your height. That happens when the top tube would stand above your inseam, or less than 80 mm of seatpost would show.
  3. The saddle height follows the 109% rule (see Saddle height). The saddle is then moved fore and aft to put your knee where the riding style wants it, within what a standard seatpost allows.
  4. The stem (near the stock length for the size) and the spacers under it are chosen to bring your back to the riding style’s angle. On drop bars they must also leave your shoulder open to at least 75°, so your reach isn’t cramped.
  5. Casual on drop bars may take the next size up, if it clears your inseam and fits better. A larger frame raises the bars without pulling them closer, which riders once called the “French fit”.

The crank length goes with the frame size for your height: 160 to 175 mm.

Riding styles

For each kind of bike, makers sell a relaxed frame and a race frame in the same sizes, and fitters set a relaxed or a race position on them. The three styles follow those differences, compared size for size on the makers’ published charts.

  • Front end height. Road race frames stand about 16 mm lower than endurance frames on small sizes, 21 mm on medium and 26 mm on large. Their stack to reach ratio averages about 1.44, against 1.53. Gravel race frames stand about 16 mm lower than adventure frames. Cross-country race hardtails stand about 20 mm lower than trail hardtails, mostly from a shorter fork, so the styles use forks of 120, 110 and 100 mm of travel.
  • Handling. Road race frames are about a degree steeper at the head tube, with about 5 mm less rake. They have 410 mm chainstays against 420 mm, a bottom bracket 3 to 4 mm higher, and 28 mm tires against 32 mm. Gravel race frames are about half a degree steeper, with 425 mm chainstays against 435 mm. Cross-country race hardtails have 425 mm chainstays against 440 mm.
  • Back angle. Fitting guides (Andy Pruitt, Phil Burt and others, hands on the hoods) put relaxed riding at about 50° to 60°, fitness and endurance riding at 40° to 50°, and racing at 30° to 45°. Gravel positions sit a little more upright than road ones. The cockpit is set to aim for these angles:

The saddle is placed to put the knee 15 mm behind the pedal axle for Casual, 5 mm behind for Balanced and 5 mm ahead for Aggressive. Endurance bikes carry more seatpost setback than race bikes, and racers often sit further forward. The stem is turned up for Casual and down for the lower styles.

Saddle height

The page shows three common ways of setting saddle height.

  • 109% of inseam (Hamley and Thomas, 1967). From the pedal axle at the bottom of the stroke, with the crank in line with the seat tube, to the top of the saddle. Set saddle height from inseam uses it, and the Pedal to saddle top readout shows it.
  • 88.3% of inseam (Greg LeMond’s Complete Book of Bicycling, 1987). From the center of the bottom bracket to the top of the saddle. The BB to saddle top readout shows it.
  • Knee angle (the Holmes method; Holmes, Pruitt and Whalen, 1994). The knee bent 25° to 35° at the bottom of the stroke, which is 145° to 155° as this page measures it. The Knee, leg extended readout uses this range, and Set saddle height from recommended leg extension aims for its middle, 150°.

Studies comparing these methods (Peveler and colleagues, 2005 and after) found that the inseam rules leave many riders outside the knee range. That is why fitters tend to go by the knee angle. Here the knee angle is measured on the moving model, with the foot angle set under Posture.

Readouts and their ranges

Green means typical, yellow marginal and red out of range. Gray labels describe without judging.

  • Knee, leg extended: 145° to 155° (see Saddle height).
  • Knee, top of stroke: 65° to 72°, the 110° to 115° of bend fitters aim for. A tighter bend usually means long cranks or a low saddle.
  • Hip, most closed: 45° to 65°, the range in published road fitting guidance. Under 45° the thigh crowds the chest.
  • Back angle: 50° and up is casual, 40° to 50° balanced, under 40° aggressive (see Riding styles).
  • Shoulder angle: 75° to 100°, the span fitters see as normal; their targets, 80° to 95°, sit inside it.
  • Knee vs pedal axle: the classic “knee over pedal spindle” starting point, from level to 20 mm behind. Fitters disagree on whether it should be a target (Keith Bontrager among the critics), so it is gray.
  • Standover height: measured halfway along the top tube, to its upper surface. 26 mm or more under your inseam is clear, 1 to 25 mm is snug, and none is too tall.
  • Saddle-to-bar drop: judged against saddle height, so tall riders aren’t marked down. Under 4% of saddle height is upright, up to 12% is moderate, and more is deep.
  • Stack to reach ratio: under 1.45 is race, 1.45 to 1.55 endurance, and over 1.55 relaxed (the line the German magazine TOUR and others draw on a medium frame). Flat-bar frames get no label.
  • Trail: 57 mm or more is stable, 50 to 56 mm responsive, and under 50 mm twitchy. Road bikes run from about 50 to 63 mm, with 57 or 58 often given as the balance point.
  • Toe overlap: the closest the shoe comes to the front tire over the stroke, seen from the side with the wheel straight.
  • Fit assessment: At risk or Does not fit while there is a warning. Otherwise it names the riding style nearest your back angle, seated with your hands on the hoods.

The ranges for trail, stack to reach and standover are road-bike ranges, so a gravel or mountain bike will often read stable or relaxed.

How the drawing works
  • To scale. Everything is worked out in millimeters and degrees from the center of the bottom bracket, and drawn to scale.
  • Frame. Stack and reach place the top of the head tube. The effective top tube is reach plus stack ÷ tan(seat tube angle). The fork, a 12 mm lower headset and the head tube add up to the stack.
  • Wheels. Wheel radius is half the rim size plus the tire width. Trail is (wheel radius × cos(head tube angle) − rake) ÷ sin(head tube angle).
  • Legs. The hip sits just above the saddle, 125 mm ahead of its tail. Each leg is solved as a two-part chain, thigh and lower leg, reaching the pedal with the foot at the angle you set. As riders do, it points the toes further before the knee locks.
  • Arms and torso. The shoulder sits where the torso’s length from the hip meets the arm’s reach to the hands, at the elbow bend you set.
  • Hills. Up to 10° the rider stays seated and leans forward. Beyond that the hips leave the saddle and move forward, into a low crouch at 30°.
  • Speed. Cadence × (chainring ÷ cog) × the wheel’s circumference.
  • Simplifications. Tubes are straight, the saddle is level, shoe and pedal height is fixed, and the drop bar is a classic round bend.
